# Evidence for a Kankō review model

Research date: 24 September 2026. Companion: [proposed review model](kanko-review-model.md).

This is a targeted literature review for a product decision, not an exhaustive systematic review or a meta-analysis. It investigates how reviewers understand changes, how presentation affects inspection, and how AI assistance affects human judgment. Sixteen core primary studies inform the proposal; one recent preprint is tracked separately. The evidence supports individual design choices to different degrees. It does not validate the complete Kankō model.

Searches covered change understanding and information needs, change decomposition, code ordering and file position, explicit review strategies, test-first review, existing review comments, anchored code explanations, and AI-assisted review and overreliance. Examples of search phrases were `code review change understanding information needs`, `Do explicit review strategies improve code review performance`, `code review file order experiment`, and `code review AI generated explanations human reviewers comprehension`.

Selection favored original empirical studies with inspectable methods and outcomes. References in the ordering and guidance papers supplied additional studies, including contradictory results. Publisher pages, author manuscripts, university repositories, and author-uploaded preprints were used. Search-engine dates were not treated as publication dates. Full-text sections were inspected where accessible; access limits are identified below. Secondary reviews helped discover papers but are not used to establish the empirical claims here. Marketing claims, automated comment-generation benchmark scores, and unverified summaries were excluded as evidence of human review quality.

The corpus is concentrated in modern code review and includes overlapping research teams. Java, large technology organizations, student populations, and short experimental tasks are prominent. Replication across languages, teams, change types, and modern models is limited. Large repository datasets offer breadth but often measure comments rather than actual reading or understanding. No included study directly tests the complete proposed Kankō tour.

**S1 — Change understanding extends beyond the edited lines.** Tao, Dang, Xie, Zhang, and Kim, *How Do Software Engineers Understand Code Changes? An Exploratory Study in Industry*, FSE 2012. A survey of 180 Microsoft participants, followed by interviews, identified rationale, completeness, consistency, and risk to other code as important information needs. Rationale was especially important; assessing wider impact was difficult. This supports including the reason for a change and its affected surroundings. It is self-reported, single-company evidence rather than a causal test of a review interface. Methods and findings were inspected in the [author manuscript](https://people.csail.mit.edu/hunkim/papers/tao-fse2012.pdf), especially Sections 3 and 5–7.

**S2 — Understanding and knowledge transfer are substantive review outcomes.** Bacchelli and Bird, *Expectations, Outcomes, and Challenges of Modern Code Review*, ICSE 2013. The study combined observations/interviews with 17 developers across 16 Microsoft teams, classification of 570 comments, and surveys. Understanding the change emerged as a central difficulty; observed review benefits extended beyond defect discovery. This justifies measuring understanding alongside inspection outcomes. It does not show that improving explanation alone improves defect detection. See the methods and change-understanding discussion in the [Microsoft manuscript](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/ICSE202013-codereview.pdf).

**S3 — Review goals vary with the working relationship.** Sadowski, Söderberg, Church, Sipko, and Bacchelli, *Modern Code Review: A Case Study at Google*, ICSE SEIP 2018. Twelve interviews, 44 survey responses, and logs for nine million reviewed changes describe a lightweight process supporting several goals, including education. This supports adapting the tour to whether someone needs orientation, inspection, or an ownership handoff. Nine million logs do not mean nine million measured comprehension outcomes, and Google's process should not be treated as universal. The study's scope and findings are available in the [Google manuscript](https://storage.googleapis.com/gweb-research2023-stg-media/pubtools/4476.pdf).

**S4 — Reviewers ask several different kinds of question.** Pascarella, Spadini, Palomba, Bruntink, and Bacchelli, *Information Needs in Contemporary Code Review*, CSCW 2018. Analysis of 900 comments from three open-source projects produced seven categories covering alternatives, understanding, rationale, context, necessity, specialist help, and separability of changes. Interviews and a focus group supplemented the analysis. This supports organizing a tour around information needs and recording unanswered questions. Comment analysis misses needs resolved silently or outside the review tool. See the taxonomy and results in the [author manuscript](https://sback.it/publications/cscw2018.pdf), particularly Figure 3 and the results sections.

**S5 — Relatedness is a stronger organizing idea than a single direction.** Baum, Schneider, and Bacchelli, *On the Optimal Order of Reading Source Code Changes for Review*, ICSME 2017. The authors developed a theory from 12 task-guided interviews, 292 recorded review sessions, a survey with varying response counts, and related literature. The theory favors grouping connected changes, supplying needed context, and making the grouping understandable. It explicitly accommodates more than one useful route. These are grounded design principles, not experimental proof of an optimal algorithm. See Sections III–IV of the [author manuscript](https://tobiasbaum.github.io/rp/optimalordering.pdf).

**S6 — Ordering theory has inconclusive experimental support.** Baum, Schneider, and Bacchelli, *Associating Working Memory Capacity and Code Change Ordering with Code Review Performance*, Empirical Software Engineering 2019. In an experiment with 50 participants, mostly professionals, working memory was moderately associated with detecting defects spread across different code locations. The experiment could not reliably establish an ordering effect on review efficiency. This constrains claims for a particular traversal and motivates keeping related evidence accessible. Tasks came from one system and included seeded defects. See the [published study](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10664-018-9676-8).

**S7 — Decomposition can reduce false alarms without improving every outcome.** di Biase, Bruntink, van Deursen, and Bacchelli, *The Effects of Change Decomposition on Code Review—A Controlled Experiment*, PeerJ Computer Science 2019. Twenty-eight professional and graduate-student developers reviewed tangled or separated changes. Decomposition reduced wrongly reported issues and increased context seeking; the study did not find an improvement in defects found or understanding of rationale. Separating PRs is also a different intervention from grouping stops inside a tour. The small, single-system setting limits generalization. Findings and design were verified through the [authors' institutional record](https://research.tudelft.nl/en/publications/the-effects-of-change-decomposition-on-code-reviewa-controlled-ex/) and indexed article text; full-text access was intermittent.

**S8 — Starting with tests changes the balance of attention.** Spadini, Palomba, Baum, Hanenberg, Bruntink, and Bacchelli, *Test-Driven Code Review: An Empirical Study*, ICSE 2019. The paper reports 93 developers in its abstract and 92 analyzed developers/154 reviews in its body, plus interviews and a survey. Test-first review found more test bugs, with fewer production maintainability issues; production bug detection did not improve. Test-first is therefore an option with tradeoffs. The study does not establish an ideal order for every change. See the experiment and conclusion in the [author manuscript](https://sback.it/publications/icse2019a.pdf) and its [author-uploaded record](https://zenodo.org/records/2551217).

**S9 — File position can affect defect detection.** Fregnan, Braz, D’Ambros, Çalikli, and Bacchelli, *First Come First Served: The Impact of File Position on Code Review*, ESEC/FSE 2022. Repository analysis covered 219,476 PRs from 138 Java projects; an experiment involved 106 participants. For one seeded corner-case defect, detection odds were 64% lower when its file appeared last. The other defect showed no position effect. This is evidence that presentation can allocate attention unevenly, not a universal 64% reduction in accuracy. See the design, results, and limitations in the [University of Glasgow manuscript](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/273443/3/273443.pdf).

**S10 — More explicit guidance is not reliably better.** Wurzel Gonçalves, Fregnan, Baum, Schneider, and Bacchelli, *Do Explicit Review Strategies Improve Code Review Performance? Towards Understanding the Role of Cognitive Load*, Empirical Software Engineering 2022. A registered experiment recruited 70 developers; 67 remained after cleaning. Most were novice reviewers. Ad hoc, checklist, and guided-checklist conditions yielded mixed, task-dependent results with weak overall support for guidance improving performance. Low performance and participant conditions constrained interpretation. This argues for testing the amount of guidance, not assuming benefit from additional steps. See Sections 4–6 of the [published paper](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10664-022-10123-8).

**S11 — Actual review behavior uses several ordering patterns.** Bouraffa, Brandt, Zaidman, and Maalej, *Not One to Rule Them All: Mining Meaningful Code Review Orders From GitHub*, EASE 2025. Analysis of 23,241 PRs in 100 Java/Python repositories found non-alphabetical comment order in 44.6% of the selected PRs, with several alternative patterns. This supports accommodating different routes. Comment order is a proxy for navigation: silent reading, previous passes, selection criteria, and reviewer differences limit the inference. It does not prove that one order causes better reviews. See Sections 2, 6, and 7 of the [author manuscript](https://azaidman.github.io/publications/bouraffaEASE2025.pdf).

**S12 — AI suggestions can concentrate human attention.** Tufano, Martin-Lopez, Tayeb, Dabić, Haiduc, and Bavota, *Deep Learning-based Code Reviews: A Paradigm Shift or a Double-Edged Sword?*, ICSE 2025; preprint 2024. In a controlled study with 29 experts and 72 analyzed reviews, AI-assisted reviewers concentrated on suggested locations and found more low-severity issues, without finding more high-severity issues or saving time. This directly motivates testing omissions and attention outside the tour's suggested path. The study used particular programs and GPT-4 assistance, not Kankō; incomplete participation also altered the planned design. See Sections III–IV of the [full manuscript](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.11401v3).

**S13 — Anchored explanations can improve measured comprehension.** Yan, Hwang, Wu, and Head, *Ivie: Lightweight Anchored Explanations of Just-Generated Code*, CHI 2024. A study with 32 programmers compared explanations near code with a chat-based baseline. Comprehension-question accuracy averaged 90.2% versus 65.0%. Participants were mainly graduate students, and tasks concerned unfamiliar APIs in short generated programs. This supports testing concise explanations connected to source. It does not establish cross-file architectural understanding, long-term retention, or PR defect detection. See Sections 5–7 of the [full manuscript](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.02491v1).

**S14 — Deliberate checking has benefits and interaction costs.** Buçinca, Malaya, and Gajos, *To Trust or to Think: Cognitive Forcing Functions Can Reduce Overreliance on AI in AI-assisted Decision-making*, CSCW 2021. An experiment with 199 participants found that interventions requiring more deliberate engagement reduced overreliance compared with simple explainable-AI conditions. The strongest interventions received poorer subjective ratings. This suggests evaluating selective checks rather than assuming either frictionless use or constant questioning is ideal. This was a non-code decision task, so applying its results to review is a transfer hypothesis. See the design and results in the [full manuscript](https://arxiv.org/html/2102.09692v1).

**S15 — Make verification less expensive.** Vasconcelos, Jörke, Grunde-McLaughlin, Gerstenberg, Bernstein, and Krishna, *Explanations Can Reduce Overreliance on AI Systems During Decision-Making*, CSCW 2023. Five studies with 731 participants used a maze task and manipulated the costs and benefits of checking AI advice. Explanations helped under some conditions; verification cost mattered. This supports testing whether nearby code and evidence make a tour's claims easier to check. It does not show that longer explanations, citations alone, or a particular code-review layout improve judgment. See the studies and discussion in the [full manuscript](https://arxiv.org/html/2212.06823v2).

**S16 — Existing feedback is not necessarily harmful priming.** Spadini, Çalikli, and Bacchelli, *Primers or Reminders? The Effects of Existing Review Comments on Code Review*, ICSE 2020. An experiment with 85 developers did not find the hypothesized broad negative effect of visible comments on finding other bug types. Comments about a less commonly considered bug could act as useful reminders. This prevents turning the AI-attention concern into a blanket rule to hide feedback or require a blind review first. The task and defect types were narrow. See Sections 5–7 of the [author manuscript](https://gulcalikli.github.io/files/priming.pdf).

**Emerging evidence — trust is not demonstrated correctness.** Gao, Muñoz Barón, Habiba, Graziotin, and Wagner, *Evaluating the Impact of Explainable AI on Trust in AI-Assisted Code Review*, July 2026 preprint. A study with 34 participants compared explanation conditions. More extensive explanations increased perceived trustworthiness, but recommendation acceptance did not follow the same pattern; time differences were not statistically significant. The inspected [preprint](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.24601v1) reinforces the need to separate trust from objective outcomes. Peer-review status was not verified, so this is a watch item, not a foundation for the model.

**The main uncertainty is the combination.** The evidence is strongest for the existence of information needs and for specific effects in particular experimental settings. It is weaker for the proposition that an adaptive, conversational, model-authored tour improves whole-system understanding and review decisions. The companion proposal turns that proposition into observable outcomes and an evaluation plan. None of the studies supplies a universal stop count, reading speed, PR size limit, or scoring formula that Kankō should adopt as established science.
